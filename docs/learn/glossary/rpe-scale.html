RPE scale — strength-training glossary

What it means

RPE stands for Rating of Perceived Exertion. In strength training, it's a numerical scale used to describe how hard a set felt relative to your maximum effort. The most common version runs from 1 to 10, where RPE 10 means you could not have completed another rep, RPE 9 means one rep left in the tank, RPE 8 means two reps left, and so on. It gives lifters a shared language for communicating training intensity without relying solely on percentages of a one-rep max.

The scale was originally developed by researcher Gunnar Borg in a 6–20 format for cardiovascular research, but the 1–10 version—popularized in powerlifting by coach Mike Tuchscherer—became the standard in strength sports. Rather than prescribing a fixed weight, a coach can program "3 sets of 5 at RPE 8," letting the lifter self-regulate based on how they actually feel that day.

Why it matters

RPE becomes especially useful on days when your body doesn't match expectations. Fatigue, sleep, nutrition, and stress all affect how heavy a given weight feels. A percentage-based program might tell you to lift 85% of your max regardless of how you feel, while an RPE-based program lets you hit the intended training stimulus even when 85% feels like 95%. It also helps beginners and intermediate lifters develop body awareness—learning to notice how close to failure they actually are, rather than guessing.

Coaches and training partners also use RPE to track fatigue accumulation over a training block. If the same weight at the same rep count starts creeping from RPE 7 to RPE 9 over several weeks, that's a reliable signal that fatigue is building and a deload may be needed.

Example

A program calls for a top set of 5 reps at RPE 8 on the squat. You work up and complete 5 reps at 225 lb, feeling like you had roughly 2 clean reps left. That's RPE 8. The following week, 225 lb moves to RPE 9—only 1 rep left—signaling the weight needs to increase or recovery needs attention.

Common confusions

RPE vs. percentage of 1RM. These systems overlap but aren't interchangeable. An RPE 8 for 5 reps corresponds roughly to 80% of max for many lifters, but individual variation is significant. Never assume a fixed percentage equals a fixed RPE.

RPE vs. RIR (Reps in Reserve). RIR is the raw number of reps you could still perform—"2 reps in reserve." RPE 8 and 2 RIR describe the same set, but from different directions. RIR counts what's left; RPE rates the effort. They're two sides of the same concept, and the terms are often used interchangeably in modern programming.

Subjective doesn't mean inaccurate. Many lifters dismiss RPE because it "feels too personal." With practice, self-reported RPE correlates reliably with actual proximity to failure and is a legitimate training tool, not just a vague feeling.
