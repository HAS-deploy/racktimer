RIR — strength-training glossary

What it means

RIR stands for Reps in Reserve — a way of measuring how close you are to failure on any given set. An RIR of 2 means you could have done 2 more reps before your form broke down or you genuinely couldn't lift the weight. An RIR of 0 means you hit absolute failure on that last rep. It's essentially the inverse of RPE (Rate of Perceived Exertion): the harder the set, the lower your RIR.

RIR is used to regulate training intensity without always pushing to failure. Instead of prescribing a fixed weight, a coach or program might tell you to work at 2–3 RIR, giving you a target zone for how challenging each set should feel — regardless of what's loaded on the bar.

Why it matters

RIR shows up most often in hypertrophy and strength programs that use autoregulation — meaning the training load adjusts based on how you're feeling on a given day rather than following a rigid percentage scheme. If you're fatigued from poor sleep or high stress, a weight that's normally 2 RIR might drop to 0 RIR; RIR-based programming tells you to reduce the load rather than grind through it.

It's also used to track progress. If a weight that used to feel like 1 RIR now feels like 3 RIR, you've gotten stronger, even if the number on the bar hasn't changed yet.

Example

You're doing a set of Romanian deadlifts for 10 reps at 135 lb. When you finish rep 10, you honestly feel like you could have cranked out 2 or 3 more clean reps. That's roughly 2–3 RIR. If your program calls for sets at "2 RIR," this set was on target. If it called for "0–1 RIR," you'd need to add weight.

Common confusions

  • RIR vs. RPE: RPE is a 1–10 scale of overall effort (RPE 8 = hard but manageable). RIR counts remaining reps directly. They're related — RPE 8 roughly corresponds to 2 RIR on most rep ranges — but they're not interchangeable, especially at low rep counts where the math breaks down.
  • RIR vs. failure training: Training at 0 RIR means hitting failure. Some people assume all effective training requires 0 RIR, but research generally supports that sets ending at 1–3 RIR produce similar hypertrophy with less accumulated fatigue.
  • Estimating RIR accurately: Beginners tend to overestimate their RIR — thinking they have 4 reps left when they actually have 1. Accurate RIR tracking is a skill that improves with experience, which is one of its main practical limitations.
