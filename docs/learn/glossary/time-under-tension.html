time under tension — strength-training glossary

What it means

Time under tension (TUT) refers to the total duration a muscle is under load during a set. It's measured in seconds and accounts for all four phases of a lift: the lowering (eccentric), the pause at the bottom, the lifting (concentric), and the pause at the top. A set of 10 reps done quickly might last 20 seconds; the same 10 reps performed with deliberate, controlled tempo might last 50 seconds.

The idea is that mechanical tension on muscle fibers is one of the primary drivers of hypertrophy, and simply counting reps doesn't capture how long those fibers are actually being challenged. Two people can do "3 sets of 10" and have very different training stimuli if their tempos are completely different.

Why it matters

TUT becomes relevant when you're trying to maximize muscle growth, break through a plateau, or train around an injury without adding more weight. Coaches often prescribe it using tempo notation — for example, 3-1-2-0, meaning a 3-second lowering, 1-second pause at the bottom, 2-second lift, and no pause at the top. This forces intentionality that rep counting alone doesn't enforce.

It also matters for program design. Beginner programs typically don't specify tempo, so lifters develop the habit of moving weight as fast as possible. Adding TUT constraints can shift a light weight into a genuinely difficult stimulus without loading the joints more heavily.

Example

A lifter does a barbell curl with a 3-0-1-0 tempo: 3 seconds to lower the bar, no pause, 1 second to curl it up, no pause. Ten reps at this tempo takes roughly 40 seconds. Compare that to bouncing through the same 10 reps in 15 seconds — same weight, same rep count, meaningfully different muscle stimulus.

Common confusions

TUT vs. slow reps: These overlap but aren't identical. Slow reps usually just mean a slower concentric or eccentric phase. TUT is a total-set metric; you can have high TUT with moderate tempo if you do more reps, or with extended pauses rather than slow movement.

TUT as the only variable that matters: Some training circles treat TUT as the master lever for hypertrophy, but it's one factor among several — load, volume, proximity to failure, and exercise selection all interact with it. Chasing a specific TUT number while using a trivially light weight won't produce the same result as working near your limit.

Confusing eccentric and concentric tempo: People sometimes apply the slow tempo to the wrong phase. The eccentric (lowering) phase tends to produce more muscle damage and is often where extending TUT pays off most, but that's frequently the phase lifters rush through.
