1 rep max — strength-training glossary

What it means

Your 1 rep max (1RM) is the maximum amount of weight you can lift for exactly one complete repetition of a given exercise with proper form. It represents your absolute strength ceiling for that movement on that day. It's specific to each exercise — your 1RM on the bench press has no direct relationship to your 1RM on the squat.

Why it matters

1RM is used as a reference point for programming training intensity. Rather than prescribing arbitrary weights, many strength programs write sets as a percentage of your 1RM — for example, "3 sets of 5 at 80%." This makes programming transferable across athletes of different strength levels. Testing or estimating your 1RM is common when running powerlifting-style programs, peaking cycles, or any protocol that uses percentage-based loading. It also serves as a straightforward benchmark for tracking progress over months or years.

Example

You want to run a squat program that calls for working up to sets at 75% and 85% of your 1RM. You test your squat and hit 200 lb for one clean rep, failing on a second attempt. Your 1RM is 200 lb. That means your 75% sets are at 150 lb and your 85% sets are at 170 lb. Every prescription in the program now has a concrete number attached to it.

Common confusions

1RM vs. estimated 1RM: Many lifters — especially beginners — never actually attempt a true single max effort because it carries injury risk and requires a lot of recovery. Instead, they use a rep-max formula (such as the Epley formula) to estimate their 1RM from a multi-rep set. If you lift 175 lb for 5 reps, the formula spits out an approximate 1RM. This estimate can be off by 5–10%, so true tested 1RMs and calculated ones aren't interchangeable.

1RM vs. training max: Some programs (like 5/3/1) deliberately use a "training max" set at 85–90% of your actual 1RM as the basis for percentages. This creates a conservative buffer that keeps training weights manageable. If someone says their training max is 225 lb, their real 1RM is likely around 250–265 lb.

1RM vs. daily readiness: Your 1RM isn't a fixed number. Sleep, stress, nutrition, and fatigue can shift it by several percent on any given day. A 1RM tested on a good day after a proper peaking cycle will typically be higher than one tested mid-program while fatigued.
