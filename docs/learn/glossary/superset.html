superset — strength-training glossary

What it means

A superset is when you perform two exercises back-to-back with little or no rest between them, then rest after completing both. The two exercises are treated as a single combined set. Supersets can pair muscles that oppose each other (antagonist supersets, like biceps curls followed by triceps extensions) or muscles that work together (agonist supersets, like two different chest exercises in a row).

Why it matters

Supersets are primarily a time-saving tool. By working one muscle group while another recovers, you cut down total workout time without sacrificing training volume. Antagonist supersets in particular can be done with minimal performance loss because the resting muscle isn't fatigued when it's called on. Agonist supersets (sometimes called compound sets) are more demanding and create more metabolic stress, making them useful for hypertrophy-focused training when that extra fatigue is intentional.

Example

You do 10 reps of barbell rows, then immediately walk to the bench and do 10 reps of bench press — no rest between the two. After finishing both exercises, you rest 90 seconds, then repeat the pairing for the next set. That pairing is one superset.

Common confusions

  • Superset vs. giant set: A giant set chains three or more exercises together. A superset is specifically two exercises. The distinction matters when reading programs, since giant sets are significantly more fatiguing.
  • Superset vs. circuit: A circuit typically strings together four or more exercises, often rotating through them for multiple rounds with a conditioning or full-body goal. Supersets are usually focused on two specific muscle groups and are embedded within a more traditional strength session.
  • Antagonist vs. agonist supersets: People often use "superset" to mean only the antagonist version (opposing muscles). In practice, any two exercises done back-to-back count, but the recovery dynamics are very different. Pairing two chest exercises back-to-back is far more tiring than pairing chest with back.
  • No rest vs. very short rest: True supersets involve moving directly from one exercise to the other without a deliberate rest break. If you're taking 30–60 seconds between the two exercises, it's closer to two separate sets done in sequence, which changes both the training effect and how much weight you can handle.
